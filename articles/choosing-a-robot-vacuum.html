How to choose a robot vacuum

Home goods · Published September 20, 2026

A robot vacuum is only as good as its fit with your home. Before comparing models, it helps to know what your floors, furniture and routine demand. After that, most of the decision comes down to navigation, brush design and how much maintenance you're willing to do.

Start with your home, not the spec sheet

Answer these before you look at a single product page:

  • Floor types. Mostly hard floors, mostly carpet, or a mix? Thick or high-pile rugs are harder for any robot, and some mopping models need to lift or remove their pads to cross carpet.
  • Layout. One open room, or many rooms with doorways? Multiple floors? A robot that maps your home is far more useful in a multi-room layout than in a studio apartment.
  • Clearance. Measure the gap under your sofa, bed and cabinets, then compare it with each model's stated height. A robot that can't get under the furniture will miss exactly the spots you most want it to clean. Some models have a raised sensor turret on top that adds height.
  • Thresholds. Check door sills and rug edges. Manufacturers usually list a maximum climbing height; compare it against your tallest threshold.
  • Pets and hair. Long hair, whether human or pet, is the main cause of tangled brushes. Pet owners should also consider obstacle avoidance (see below).
  • Clutter. Cables, socks and toys on the floor can stop a robot or get pulled into the brush. Be honest about how much tidying you'll do before each run.

Navigation: the biggest difference between models

How a robot finds its way around decides how thoroughly and efficiently it cleans, and whether features like room-by-room cleaning are possible at all.

Navigation typeHow it worksGood forWatch out for
Bump-and-turn (random) Moves until it hits something, then changes direction. Single small rooms and tight budgets. Covers ground unevenly, takes longer, usually no map or room selection.
Gyroscope / inertial Tracks its own movement to clean in rows. Simple layouts where you want tidier coverage than random models. Limited or no saved maps; can lose its place in complex homes.
Camera-based (vSLAM) Uses a camera to recognize landmarks and build a map. Multi-room homes; often paired with visual obstacle recognition. Can struggle in dim light; a camera in your home raises privacy questions.
Lidar / laser A spinning or solid-state laser sensor measures distances to build a map. Multi-room homes; works in the dark; tends to map quickly and accurately. A raised turret can add height; lidar alone doesn't identify small objects like cables.

If you want to send the robot to a particular room, set no-go zones or clean only the kitchen after dinner, you need a model that creates and saves a map. If you have more than one floor, check whether it can store more than one map.

Obstacle avoidance

Mapping tells the robot where the walls are. Obstacle avoidance is about small things on the floor: cords, shoes, pet accidents. Models use structured light, cameras, or both to detect and steer around objects. How well this works varies, and it's hard to judge from marketing claims, so look for independent tests or detailed owner reports. If your floor is often cluttered, or you have a pet, it's worth paying more for this feature.

Suction numbers: treat them with caution

Many manufacturers advertise suction in pascals (Pa). Those figures are useful for comparing models within one brand's lineup, but they are not measured to a common, independently verified standard across brands, and a higher number doesn't necessarily mean more dirt picked up. How well a robot cleans also depends on brush design, how well it seals against the floor, airflow path and navigation. Where independent pickup tests exist, they tell you more than a headline suction number.

Brushes and hair tangles

  • Main brush. Rubber or rubber-and-bristle rollers generally tangle less with hair than all-bristle brushes and are easier to clean. Some designs are marketed specifically as anti-tangle; if hair is a concern, look for owner feedback on how well that holds up.
  • Side brushes. These sweep debris out of corners and along edges. One or two is typical. They wear out and are a regular replacement part.
  • Brush removal. Check that the main brush pops out without tools. You'll be doing it regularly.

Self-emptying docks

A self-emptying dock pulls debris out of the robot's small onboard bin into a larger bag or container in the base, so you empty it far less often. It's one of the most useful upgrades, especially with pets, but consider the trade-offs:

  • Space. The dock is much larger than a basic charger and needs clear space around it.
  • Noise. Emptying is loud for a few seconds. Most apps let you schedule it or turn it off during certain hours.
  • Bags. Many docks use disposable bags, which are an ongoing cost. Some are bagless, but you then have to empty and clean a container.
  • Compatibility. Check whether bags come from the manufacturer only, and whether they're easy to buy where you live.

Mopping: vacuum-mop combos

Mopping features range from a damp cloth dragged behind the robot to rotating pads with docks that wash and dry them. The basic drag-pad designs are best thought of as a light daily wipe, not a replacement for proper mopping. If you want mopping, look at:

  • Carpet handling. Does it lift the mop pads, detach them, or avoid carpet altogether? A robot with no carpet strategy will either skip your rugs or wet them.
  • Pad cleaning. Docks that wash and dry pads save work but are larger and need their water tanks filled and emptied. Dirty-water tanks and pad trays need regular cleaning to avoid odors.
  • Floor compatibility. Check your flooring manufacturer's guidance on wet cleaning, particularly for unsealed wood.

If you rarely mop or have mostly carpet, a vacuum-only model is simpler and cheaper to own.

App, schedules and smart-home features

Features that make a real difference day to day:

  • Scheduled cleaning, including different schedules for different rooms.
  • No-go zones and virtual walls (a map-based alternative to physical barrier strips).
  • Room-by-room cleaning order and settings such as suction level per room.
  • Offline or local control, if you don't want the robot to depend on the manufacturer's cloud service.

Voice assistant support is nice to have, but it's rarely a reason to pick one model over another.

Privacy

A mapping robot creates a floor plan of your home, and camera-equipped models capture images as they move around. Before buying, read the manufacturer's privacy policy and app permissions. Look for answers to these questions:

  • Are maps and images stored on the device, in the cloud, or both?
  • Can you delete your map and data?
  • Can you use the robot without creating an account or connecting it to the internet, and which features stop working if you do?
  • Does the manufacturer have a track record of security updates?

Running costs and longevity

The purchase price is only part of the cost. Over the life of the robot, expect to replace:

  • Filters, side brushes and main brushes.
  • Dock bags (for self-emptying models) and mop pads (for mopping models).
  • Eventually, the battery. Batteries lose capacity with age.

Before buying, check that the manufacturer sells these parts, what they cost, and whether the battery can be replaced by the owner. Brands that publish parts lists and repair guides tend to be easier to keep running. Also check the warranty length and whether it covers the battery.

Quick checklist

  • Fits under your furniture and over your thresholds.
  • Maps your home, if you have more than one room you want to target.
  • Tangle-resistant, tool-free main brush if you have pets or long hair in the house.
  • Self-emptying dock only if you have space and accept the cost of bags.
  • Mopping only if it has a sensible way to handle your rugs.
  • Acceptable privacy policy and data controls.
  • Replacement parts and battery available.

Bottom line

For a small, simple space, a basic robot without mapping may be all you need. For a multi-room home, mapping navigation is the feature most worth paying for, followed by good obstacle avoidance if your floors are often cluttered. Self-emptying docks and mopping systems add convenience but also cost, size and maintenance, so only pay for them if you'll use them.
