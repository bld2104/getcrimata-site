What to look for in an air fryer

Home goods · Published September 20, 2026

An air fryer is a small, powerful convection oven: a heating element and a fan circulate hot air around food in a compact chamber. Knowing that makes it easier to see past the marketing and focus on what makes one model easier to live with than another.

First, decide what you'll cook

The right air fryer depends on how you'll use it:

  • How many people? Cooking for one or two is a different job from feeding a family in one batch.
  • What food? Fries, wings and frozen snacks suit a basket you can shake. Whole fish, toast, pizza or baking suit a flatter, oven-style interior.
  • Replacing or adding? If it might replace a toaster or countertop oven, a larger oven-style unit may earn its space. If it's an extra, a compact basket model may be enough.
  • Counter space. Where will it live, and will it fit under your wall cabinets with room to vent?

Styles compared

StyleStrengthsTrade-offs
Single basket Compact, simple, easy to shake food mid-cook, usually quick to heat. Limited floor area; larger batches need cooking in rounds.
Dual basket Two foods at once with separate settings; many can sync so both finish together. Each basket is narrower, so large items may not fit; wider footprint.
Oven style (door and racks) Flat shelves for toast, pizza and baking; often doubles as a countertop oven; you can see the food through the door. Bigger footprint; racks and interior take more cleaning; food on lower racks may cook unevenly without rotating.
Lid or add-on for another appliance Saves space if you already own a compatible multicooker. Often less convenient than a dedicated unit; check compatibility carefully.

How to read capacity claims

Air fryer capacity is usually quoted as volume in quarts or liters. This is one of the most misleading numbers on the box, for two reasons:

  • Air fryers cook best in a single layer. Hot air needs to reach every surface to crisp food. Piling food up tends to give uneven, softer results. What really limits a batch is the floor area of the basket or tray, not its total volume.
  • Volume isn't measured consistently. Manufacturers don't all measure it the same way, so two models with the same quoted capacity can have noticeably different usable space.

If the manufacturer lists the internal basket or tray dimensions, use those. A practical test: imagine the largest thing you cook regularly, such as a chicken, a fish fillet or a frozen pizza, and check whether it will fit flat.

Power and your kitchen circuit

Air fryers draw a lot of power while they run. Check the wattage on the label or spec sheet and think about what else shares that kitchen circuit, such as a kettle, toaster or microwave. Running several high-draw appliances together can trip a breaker.

Most manuals warn against using an extension cord. Check the power cord length as well, and make sure it will reach an outlet from where you plan to put the air fryer.

Clearance and ventilation

Air fryers push hot air out, usually from the back or top. The manual will specify how much clear space is needed around the unit. Check this before buying, especially if you plan to put it under wall cabinets. Hot exhaust can damage cabinet finishes over time.

Coatings and materials

Most baskets and trays have a nonstick coating. The main types are:

  • PTFE-based nonstick (the traditional type). It is effective and easy to clean, but it shouldn't be overheated or scratched. Follow the manufacturer's maximum temperature and avoid metal utensils and abrasive scrubbers. You'll often see "PFOA-free" on packaging. PFOA was a chemical formerly used in making PTFE, and major manufacturers phased it out years ago, so that label on its own tells you little.
  • Ceramic-type nonstick. Marketed as an alternative to PTFE. It can be less durable, and its nonstick performance tends to decline with use.
  • Stainless steel or glass. These have no coating to wear off, but food sticks more easily and cleaning takes more effort.

With any coating, check whether replacement baskets or trays are sold, since they're usually the first part to wear out. Also check whether they're dishwasher-safe. Even if they are, some manufacturers recommend hand-washing to prolong the coating's life.

Controls and features

These are the features that make a difference in everyday use:

  • Accurate temperature and timer control. Being able to set the temperature in small steps is more useful than a long list of presets.
  • Readable, easy-to-clean controls. Physical dials are simple and durable. Touch panels are easy to wipe clean but can be harder to use with wet or greasy hands.
  • Shake reminders for basket models.
  • A window or light, so you can check food without interrupting cooking.
  • Pause when opened. Most basket models pause when the drawer is pulled out; check how easily it resumes.

Features that are less important than they seem: dozens of presets (you'll use a handful), and app connectivity, which adds little for most people and depends on the manufacturer continuing to support the app.

Noise

The fan makes an air fryer noticeably noisy while it runs, and noise levels vary between models. Manufacturers rarely publish noise figures, so if this matters to you, for example in an open-plan space, look for independent measurements or detailed owner reports.

Cleaning

Cleaning is where many air fryers become a chore. Look at:

  • How many parts there are to wash: basket, crisper plate, racks, drip tray.
  • Whether the crisper plate has deep grooves or rubber bumpers that trap grease.
  • Whether you can reach the heating element to wipe off splatter (after it has cooled and been unplugged). A protective guard over the element helps, but check it isn't hard to clean.
  • For oven styles, whether the interior walls and door glass are easy to reach.

Air fryer or convection oven?

If you already own an oven with a convection (fan) setting, it can do much of what an air fryer does, with more space. What an air fryer mainly offers is a small chamber with strong airflow that heats up quickly, which is handy for small batches and quick snacks. If you regularly cook large amounts, a countertop convection oven or your main oven may suit you better than a large air fryer.

Quick checklist

  • Basket floor area (not only volume) fits the foods you cook most.
  • Style matches your cooking: basket for shaking, oven style for flat foods and baking.
  • Fits your counter, with the clearance the manual requires.
  • Suits your kitchen circuit and reaches an outlet without an extension cord.
  • Coating and materials you're comfortable with; replacement parts available.
  • Simple, precise controls and a design that's easy to clean.

Bottom line

Pick the style that fits what you cook, size it by usable floor area rather than headline capacity, and prioritize easy cleaning and precise controls over long feature lists. For most people, a well-designed, simple air fryer that is easy to clean will get far more use than a larger one packed with presets.
